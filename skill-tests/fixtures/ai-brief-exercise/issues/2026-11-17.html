#1Agents meet money, and incumbents push back

industryNov 13–17

Several things happened at once:

  • ChatGPT Wallet (Nov 16). You add a card once and approve each purchase with biometrics. The card network issues a temporary substitute credential, so neither the agent nor the merchant sees the real card number. It's built with Amex, Mastercard and Visa and is limited to US Pro subscribers. Spending-limit permissions are planned but not yet available. OpenAI's page blocked us; details are from The Neuron
  • JPMorgan draws a line. Across the five biggest US banks, agents mostly serve employees, and humans review their output. In October Dimon said third-party agents will not be allowed to operate customer accounts, citing "liability transfer." JPMorgan also links a 20% lift in private-banking gross sales to AI tools.
  • Fed Governor Barr (Nov 16) warned that autonomous agents could move deposits quickly in response to market or rate signals and speed up bank runs. He called for technical limits on what agents are allowed to do. Bloomberg is paywalled; this is confirmed only through a secondary outlet
  • Sierra's Poppy 1.0 (Nov 13) is a protocol where a business declares which agent is acting for whom and what it may do. Shopify has enabled it for all stores, and Mercury lets agents check balances and start payments under its existing approval rules. The spec is still a draft despite the "1.0" label.
Why it matters to you: The industry is converging on a trust model with three parts: tokenized credentials, a human approval at each step that moves money, and an explicit list of what an agent may do. That's the same design space as your finance assistant's guardrails, and "how would you let an agent touch a bank account safely?" is now a realistic interview question for an FDE or a fintech ML role. The answer the big banks have chosen for now is internal-first with a human in the loop. That's the safest pattern to propose at Wio (see #13).

#2Uber's blueprint for securing autonomous agents

industryNov 15

Uber described a security framework for agents in three layers:

  • Before deployment: an AI Registry records each agent's identity, owner, permitted intents and risk class. A scanner called Argus checks code, prompts, configs, MCP servers and tools.
  • At runtime: "behavioral authorization." Before an agent takes an action, AI Guards check it against policy through hooks.
  • After deployment: unified traces, which can be replayed when a policy changes.

Uber says the hard parts are false positives, added latency and friction for developers.

Why it matters to you: Most guardrails filter the input or the output. Uber's guards check actions: does this tool call fit what the user asked for? The trace-replay idea doubles as a regression test. When you tighten a guardrail, you replay last month's traffic and see what would now be blocked. You could build a small version of this around your finance assistant's tool calls: a FastAPI middleware plus a YAML registry.

#3Fable 5.2 and Gemini 4 Carbon: compare models on accuracy per dollar

releaseNov 9–14

Claude Fable 5.2 (released Nov 9, model id claude-fable-5-2) remains the reference model this week. Anthropic's own benchmarks show gains over Opus 5.5: FrontierSWE v2 69.9% vs 62.3%, and Remote Labor Index 41.3% vs 21.3%. It costs $8/$40 per million input/output tokens, or $16/$80 in fast mode, which is up to 2.5× faster. The release also adds Advisor and Orchestrator roles, where Fable advises smaller models or hands work off to them.

Meanwhile Gemini 4 Carbon (released Nov 10) took first place on MathArena:

ModelMathArenaEval cost
Gemini 4 Carbon (high)85.4% ± 3.0$15.70
Claude Fable 5.2 (max)82.8% ± 5.3$40.20
GPT-6.1 Sol (max)80.6% ± 2.7$1.07

Look at the error bars: these three models are statistically close, yet their costs differ by up to 40×. Carbon also has a common failure where it runs out of time. In one case it ran a script for more than 11 hours without checking on its progress.

Why it matters to you: Picking a model in production is a cost/accuracy/latency decision, not a leaderboard lookup. Anthropic's advisor pattern turns this into a product feature: a cheap model handles routine turns and escalates hard ones. You can apply that to your finance assistant directly. Most "what did I spend on groceries?" questions don't need a frontier model.

#4Meituan's LongCat-2.5: a 1.6-trillion-parameter open-weight agent model, trained without Nvidia GPUs

releaseNov 15

LongCat-2.5 is a mixture-of-experts model with 1.6T total parameters, of which about 48B are active per token. It takes 1M tokens of input, produces up to 128K of output, and its weights are MIT-licensed. The interesting part is the training recipe. RL tasks were built from real GitHub pull requests, with internet and git-history access blocked so the model couldn't look up the answer. An LLM judge checked for shortcuts. A 4-bit version runs on Huawei Ascend chips. The API accepts both OpenAI and Anthropic request formats, so Claude Code can point at it. Benchmarks are Meituan's own (Terminal Bench v2.1: 88.4).

Why it matters to you: The anti-shortcut judge is the RL version of a problem you'll hit in any eval: models game the metric. "Block the leak, then audit the trajectory" applies to your own eval sets too.

#5Claude Code Code Mode: the model writes JavaScript that makes the tool calls

workflowv2.1.323+, Nov 12experimental

Normally Claude makes one tool call per step. In Code Mode it writes a JS snippet that runs several calls in one step: parallel commands, filtering output before reading it, chaining results. This "code as action" pattern cuts round trips and tokens on search-then-edit tasks. It only works in the interactive terminal, not in Desktop, VS Code, -p or the Agent SDK. You need a direct Anthropic account and Opus/Sonnet/Haiku 5.5 or Fable 5.2. Permission rules and PreToolUse hooks still apply to each call.

10-minute try (in Terminal, inside any project):

claude update          # need 2.1.323 or later
CLAUDE_CODE_EXPERIMENTAL_CODE_MODE=1 claude
# accept "Yes, turn on Code Mode", then ask:
# "Read every Markdown file in this project and list links
#  to local files that don't exist."
# press Ctrl+O to see the JavaScript it wrote
Why it matters to you: Code as action is a key agent-design idea. Writing a program is often a better plan than taking one step at a time. Watching it work teaches you the pattern faster than reading about it.

#8db-branch: a disposable copy of the database for every agent run

buildShow HN, ~Nov 15
  • Base: a ZFS dataset holding a loaded Postgres or MySQL database.
  • Branch: a ZFS clone of a snapshot. Copy-on-write means no data is copied, so a branch is ready in seconds.
  • Serving: each branch runs its own database server on its own port, and ProxySQL can route queries by user.
  • Interfaces: a CLI, a REST API, a web console and an MCP server, all on the same API.
  • Limits (from HN): no merging changes back, memory grows with the number of branches, and the base must be shut down cleanly before snapshotting. Alternatives: Neon branching, Postgres 18 file_copy_method=clone, or a SQLite file per branch.
Steal this: The answer to "how do I let an agent touch production-like data safely?" is to give each run its own throwaway copy and delete it afterward. For a portfolio version, a pytest fixture that hands each agent eval run a fresh SQLite copy gives you 80% of the idea. You can try it without installing anything at demo.db-branch.com.

Sources: GitHub · HN discussion

#9Pulsar: messaging between Claude Code, Codex and Cursor

buildShow HN, ~Nov 16early (25★)
  • A small daemon on each machine keeps a registry of agents and logs messages to SQLite.
  • Each agent's MCP server is a thin client that talks to the daemon over a Unix socket only the user can access.
  • Messages are delivered through each agent's own mechanism, such as Claude Code's inbox socket or codex queue --resume. Where there's no way to push, they wait in an inbox.
Steal this: Thin MCP clients in front of one local daemon is a clean way to share state across tools. Also notice the new attack surface: a message from another agent is untrusted input, which is the same problem as #11. Run npx pulsar setup --dry-run first, because setup edits your clients' config files.

Concept of the day

#11Provenance guardrails: ask where a value came from, not whether it looks right · Track A, LLM engineering

Why today: Three items this week share one idea. Uber checks actions (#2). The SPILLWAY paper (arXiv 2611.07222, updated Nov 16) only allows a tool call if every target value came from a trusted source. And practitioners are writing about guardrails for financial assistants that check numbers against tool output.

Intuition

A model's answer mixes two kinds of tokens: values it copied from somewhere, and text it generated. Most guardrails ask a second model, "does this look wrong?" That check is probabilistic, costs money, and is easy to fool. A provenance guardrail asks something cheaper and deterministic: "where did this value come from?" If a balance in the answer didn't come from a tool result, it doesn't ship, however plausible it looks.

Mechanism

Label every value by its source, the way security people do taint tracking, and write a policy for what each source is allowed to do:

Source of valueCan appear in the answer?Can be an argument to a tool call that changes something?
User's own requestYesYes (this is the task)
Trusted read-only tool, typed field (e.g. balance: float)YesYes, if a delegation rule allows it
Untrusted tool text (emails, web pages, another agent's message)Quoted onlyNever. This is how prompt injection redirects payments
Computed by your code (sum, % change)Yes, via a declared derivationCase by case
Produced only by the modelNo, for numbersNo

For number fidelity, that becomes:

  1. Extract every number from the answer.
  2. Normalize each one: AED 1,234.50, 1234.5 and 1.2K are the same value at different precision. 12% could mean 0.12.
  3. Match each number to a value in the tool outputs, or to a value your code derived and logged.
  4. If anything can't be matched, block the answer or regenerate it.

The key design move is never letting the model do arithmetic you'll display. Compute derived values in code, pass them in as tool output, and let the model only copy them.

Common mistakes

  • Derived values. The model writes "spending up 14%," and 14 isn't in any tool output. One practitioner reported exactly this as their remaining failure. The fix is to compute the derived value in code.
  • Harmless numbers. Dates, "3 accounts," "step 2." A version that's too strict blocks everything. Whitelist structural numbers by context, or tag them as safe.
  • Rounding. The tool returns 1234.567 and the model writes 1,234.57. You need a tolerance tied to the number of decimal places shown, not exact string matching.
  • Using an LLM as the guard. It's fine as a second layer, but if it's the only one, your guardrail is as non-deterministic as the thing it guards.
Exercise (40 min, you write it): provenance.py

Write check_numbers(answer: str, tool_outputs: list[dict], derived: dict[str, float]) -> Report. Report lists each number found in the answer, its matched source (a tool field path or a derivation name) or UNSUPPORTED, and an overall ok: bool.

Done when these tests pass:
  • "Your balance is AED 12,450.00" with tool output {"balance": 12450} → ok, source balance.
  • "You spent 1,234.57" with {"spent": 1234.567} → ok (rounding tolerance).
  • "Spending rose 14%" with no derivation → UNSUPPORTED. With derived={"pct_change": 0.1402} → ok.
  • "Across 3 accounts on 2026-11-15" → the 3 and the date don't trigger a block (decide your rule and document it).
  • "1.2K" and "$1,200" both match 1200 at the precision shown.
  • Runs in under 5 ms per answer and uses no LLM calls.

Stretch: run it on 20 real (anonymized) assistant answers and count false blocks. Then plug it in behind Datasette Agent (#6).

Interview question: "Your LLM assistant occasionally shows wrong balances. Walk me through how you'd detect it, prevent it, and prove to compliance it's fixed." (A strong answer covers provenance checks, computing in code, an eval set of adversarial cases, and monitoring the block rate in production.)

Paper, explained

#12SWE-CoWork: coding agents pass the tests but don't finish the job

paperarXiv 2611.20218 · Nov 15

Problem. Coding benchmarks hand the agent a clear spec. Real work doesn't. Requirements are spread across people who each know part of the picture.

Idea. There are 101 tasks from 101 repos in 11 languages. The agent works inside a simulated organization of 26 colleagues, each holding partial knowledge. It has to find out the requirements, resolve conflicts between colleagues, and pass hidden tests.

Result. Across 11 models, the best two pass about 63% of the individual failing tests they're asked to fix, but complete under 8% of full assignments. Claude Opus 5.5 asks the right colleague about 93% of the time but captures only about 64% of the requirements. About 31% of requirements that agents report as done fail their tests.

Catch. The colleagues are simulated, so real people are messier and slower. It's also one benchmark from one team.

Would I use it? As a mental model, yes. Even when the agent asks the right person, it misses about a third of what they need. Agents asking for requirements is now about as good as answering them, and tracking requirements is the weak link. That's the job of a forward-deployed engineer, which is the human part that's hard to automate.

#13Map our AI systems to the CBUAE guidance note, then ship two quick controls

Problem. The UAE Central Bank's AI/ML guidance note (issued Feb 2026, per The National) expects several things: a documented governance framework with senior accountability, non-discriminatory outcomes, telling customers where AI is used, and customers' ability to ask for a human review or an explanation of AI-driven decisions. The US Fed is now discussing limits on what agents may do (#1), and big banks are keeping agents internal with humans reviewing their output. Regulatory expectations are tightening, and evidence of controls takes time to build.

Approach. Write a one-page control map: each principle in the note → the control we have today for (a) the XGBoost credit model and (b) the LLM finance assistant → the gap. Then close the two cheapest gaps:

  • Reason codes for credit decisions from SHAP, stored with each decision so an explanation request can be answered.
  • An escalate-to-human path in the assistant, plus logging of every number-provenance check (#11) as evidence of control.

2-week pilot. Week 1: the control map, reviewed with compliance. Week 2: SHAP reason codes on a backtest sample, and the assistant's escalation path behind a feature flag.

Metric. The share of principles with a documented control (target 100%). How long it takes to answer an explanation request. The assistant's unsupported-number rate.

Guardrail. Confirm with compliance whether the note is treated as binding. Sources disagree, and the original PDF link was broken when we checked. Don't present SHAP values to customers as causal.

Evidence: The National (CBUAE note) · The Asian Banker (FAB: over 70% faster trade-document processing, 6,000+ employee-built agents)

Daily-life AI habit

#14Tell Claude it owns the code, then measure what changes

workflowNov 10

Anthropic's prompting guide for Fable 5.2, relayed by Simon Willison, says the model tolerates duplicated code when ownership of the codebase is unstated. When you say it's the owner, it consolidates duplicates and sometimes makes related fixes. In Anthropic's small tests, consolidation went from under 10% of runs to about 75%. The sentence they suggest:

You are the owner and long-term maintainer of this codebase, and its design is up to you.
  1. Pick a repo with obvious duplication. Plotted's scrapers or MarketLens's old code are good candidates.
  2. Ask for a small feature twice: once without the sentence and once with it in CLAUDE.md. Use a fresh session each time.
  3. Compare git diff --stat. Did it consolidate? Did it touch files you didn't ask about?
  4. Keep the sentence for refactoring sessions and drop it for "change exactly this" tasks. Broader ownership means a wider scope of changes.

Treat this as a habit: a prompting tip is a hypothesis until you've A/B-tested it on your own work.

FDE & career corner

#15The FDE market is real. Here's what postings actually ask for

Signal. Lightcast data (via Fortune, Sep 3) shows "forward-deployed" job postings up more than 1,000% from January to August 2026 versus the same months a year earlier, against 13% growth for tech postings overall. The median advertised salary is over $188K, versus about $145K for software engineers. An analysis of about 1,000 FDE postings found Python in 66%, TypeScript in 35%, AI agents in 35%, LLM experience in 31% and RAG in 12%. It also found that about 60% are "Builder FDE" roles, and that the emphasis is on GenAI and agents more than classic ML.

Your read. You already cover Python, production ML, data pipelines and TypeScript (Plotted). The visible gap is a public agent/RAG project with evals. The deferred SEC-filings RAG with number-fidelity guardrails would close it, and #11 is its first building block.

Skill: getting the requirements out of people. #12 shows that even the best agents ask the right person and still miss about a third of the requirements. Humans miss them too. What sets FDEs apart is closing the loop: restate the requirements, get them confirmed, and track them.

Micro-practice (today). Before your next task at work, write: (1) the request in one sentence, (2) three questions whose answers would change your approach, (3) what "done" means as checkable criteria. Send (3) to the person who asked. Do this for a week and count how often their reply changed something.
